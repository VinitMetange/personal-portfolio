The Autonomy Gap: What's Actually Blocking Telecom's Level 4 Moment

Vinit Metange · ~10 min read 3GPP Rel-19 O-RAN WG2/WG3 TM Forum IG1251

TL;DR

At DTW Ignite 2026, every major vendor showed agentic AI healing 5G networks autonomously. Meanwhile, NVIDIA's own 2026 survey shows 88% of operators are still at TM Forum autonomy Level 3 or below. Three structural blockers — data fragmentation, the Near-RT inference constraint, and the exception-boundary measurement problem — explain exactly why. This edition maps each blocker and what resolving it actually takes.

The Lead

What DTW Ignite Didn't Put on the Main Stage

DTW Ignite 2026 in Copenhagen was the industry's loudest declaration yet that agentic AI has arrived in telecom. NVIDIA anchored its presence around a four-layer autonomous network stack: NeMo Safe Synthesizer for privacy-preserving synthetic training data, NemoClaw agent blueprints with OpenShell policy guardrails, GPU-accelerated RAN digital twins running at 200× CPU simulation throughput, and NV-Tesseract for real-time anomaly detection. SoftBank, KDDI, Amdocs, Tata Consultancy Services, and AdaptKey were all named partners.

Bharti Airtel provided the headline stat: 30–50% reduction in mean time to repair (MTTR) and 20–25% reduction in operating expenses from agentic fault management. Real numbers from a real Tier-1 operator.

What was not highlighted: NVIDIA's own 2026 State of AI in Telecommunications survey, released the same week, found that 88% of operators are still at TM Forum Autonomous Networks Levels 1–3. At Level 3, systems detect, correlate, and recommend — but a human approves remediation before any action executes. Only 12% operate at Level 4 or above, where defined playbooks run without per-incident human sign-off.

The demos were real. The gap those demos paper over is equally real.

Deep Dive

Three Blockers Keeping Operators Below Level 4

Level 4 autonomy is not a capability problem. The vendors have working AI. The blockers are structural, and they sit one layer below anything a keynote will surface.

1

Data Fragmentation Makes the RCA Agent Unreliable

Ericsson's agentic network operations architecture (June 2026) describes a sound five-component stack: streaming data ingestion from network, IT, and business systems; an anomaly detection layer; specialised agents for root cause analysis (RCA), impact assessment, recommendation, and actuation; a graph-based knowledge base capturing topology and historical tickets; and a human chatbot for validation before execution. The architecture is correct. The bottleneck is the data foundation.

A typical Tier-1 operator runs 15–20 distinct OSS/BSS systems spanning fault management, performance management, network inventory, service assurance, order management, revenue assurance, and fraud management. TM Forum's Open API catalog — TMF641 for service activation, TMF654 for performance management, TMF630 for rule management — provides the data model to unify them. But API coverage across the full TM Forum surface remains partial in most production environments. Legacy systems predate the TMF API era and carry real integration debt.

When the RCA agent receives a P1 alarm, it needs correlated data from the NMS, the service inventory, the SLA tracker, and the configuration management database — all in real time, with consistent entity identifiers. If any feed is stale, missing, or uses a divergent naming scheme for the same network element, the agent produces a plausible but wrong root cause. A wrong root cause at Level 4 means an autonomous action that worsens the outage. That is why Airtel's deployment, impressive as the MTTR numbers are, still runs humans in the loop for actuation: data quality is not yet consistent enough to trust autonomous execution across all fault scenarios.

2

The Near-RT Timing Constraint Limits What Agents Can Do in the RAN

Agentic use cases touching the radio access network operate inside O-RAN's timing architecture. The Near-RT RAN Intelligent Controller (Near-RT RIC) manages closed-loop control over the E2 interface at 10 ms to 1 second timescales. xApps on the Near-RT RIC must complete their entire inference cycle inside the 10 ms budget.

A July 2026 paper (arXiv:2607.01583) validated AI inference inside a live OAI/FlexRIC testbed: logistic regression at 1–5 µs, MLP at 10–25 µs, end-to-end loop latency under 4 ms for 95% of executions. Models hit 0.88–0.90 classification accuracy across MAC, RLC, PDCP, and GTP layer features.

That is the good news for narrow inference. The hard constraint: a multi-step agentic chain that queries a knowledge base, runs tool calls, and formulates a policy recommendation runs in hundreds of milliseconds to seconds — not 10 ms. LLM-based agents cannot run inside the Near-RT RIC.

The correct architecture splits the responsibility. Agentic reasoning lives in the Non-RT RIC (O-RAN WG2, rApp territory, where the A1 interface carries policy downward with latency above 1 second). The agent reasons and generates policy. The xApp executes that policy in near-real-time over the E2 interface. Most vendor demonstrations are still eliding this distinction, showing "AI in the RAN" without specifying which half of the O-RAN control loop they mean. An engineer building toward Level 4 needs to know exactly which half.

3

The Exception Boundary Is the Unsolved Engineering Problem

AdaptKey's KeySmith platform is among the more technically honest treatments of this problem at DTW Ignite. It does not route every action through a human; it routes exceptions and novel patterns through a human while known-good playbooks execute autonomously. That is the right model for Level 4.

The engineering problem is defining where that boundary sits — and having the instrumentation to know whether the AI's decisions on either side of it are actually reliable. Most operators today measure MTTR and aggregate OpEx. What they do not consistently measure: per-playbook autonomous action error rate, model confidence calibration (whether the model accurately signals uncertainty before acting), and decision-level attribution when autonomous action makes an incident worse.

Without those metrics, the exception boundary defaults to "escalate everything outside the exact known-good set" — which operationally is still Level 3 with more infrastructure.

One Thing to Know

The Autonomy Level Is Not a Single Number

The TM Forum autonomy level is not a single number for your whole network. IG1251 v3.0 supports per-domain scoring. An operator can be at Level 4 in energy efficiency — automated energy-saving xApps executing over the E2 interface with no human approval — while remaining at Level 2 in security fault remediation, where AI recommends and a human decides. When a vendor claims they can get you to Level 4, the qualifying question is: Level 4 in which domain, across what blast radius, with what playbook coverage? That question separates vendors selling a level from vendors selling a capability.

Vinit's Take

Where Real Separation Gets Built

Editor's perspective

The most useful thing a telecom PM or network engineer can do right now is not evaluate AI platforms. It is defining the exception boundary for their first autonomous action loop, and building the instrumentation to know when it is safe to widen. Operators who can precisely answer "which playbooks can execute without human approval, and how do we measure decision quality at the playbook level" are 18 months ahead of everyone still asking "which vendor has the best model." The AI is converging across vendors. The data unification, governance work, and per-playbook quality measurement is where real separation gets built — and none of it fits in a DTW demo.

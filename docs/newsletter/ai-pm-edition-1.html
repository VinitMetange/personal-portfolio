The Infrastructure Problem Is Solved. Your Agent Eval Pipeline Is Not.

Vinit Metange · ~9 min read Anthropic Evals Framework MCP July 28 RC Claude Opus 5

Claude Opus 5 and the MCP July 28 final specification removed the two biggest infrastructure constraints on agentic AI in three weeks. The model cost problem and the enterprise connectivity problem are mostly solved. The production failure is now different: most teams ship agents without a single eval covering production-representative tasks, and they learn the failure mode from users. Gartner predicts over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end of 2027 — based on a poll of more than 3,400 organizations, citing escalating costs, unclear business value, and inadequate risk controls. The reason is not infrastructure. The bottleneck is eval discipline.

The Lead

The Infrastructure Narrative Has Won. The Eval Problem Hasn't Been Touched.

Two significant things happened in the past three weeks that should update your 2026 agent roadmap.

On July 24, Anthropic shipped Claude Opus 5: frontier-class agentic reasoning and coding at $5 per million input tokens, the same price as Opus 4.8, within 0.5% of Claude Fable 5 on CursorBench 3.2. On July 28, the Model Context Protocol shipped its most significant revision since launch. The new stateless protocol core means MCP servers can now run behind a plain round-robin load balancer without sticky sessions, shared session stores, or deep packet inspection at the gateway. The authorization layer now aligns with OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect. A 12-month deprecation guarantee gives enterprise engineering teams the stability to build on it.

Both are real progress. The model problem is mostly solved. The enterprise connectivity problem is mostly solved.

Here is the contrarian position: building connectivity to your business systems is not what is killing your agents in production. Gartner predicts over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end of 2027. Not because the models are incapable. Not because MCP is not ready. Because most teams ship agents without being able to answer a basic question: what does "working" look like for this agent, at the task level, before any user sees it?

The production crisis in 2026 is not infrastructure. It is eval discipline.

Deep Dive

What the Anthropic Eval Framework Reveals About Your Production Gap

In January 2026, Anthropic published "Demystifying Evals for AI Agents" on their engineering blog. It is the clearest practitioner framework for agent evaluation published to date. Seven months after publication, most teams building production agents have not applied its central lessons.

The mechanism. Anthropic's framework defines the evaluation vocabulary that most teams are missing. A task is a test case with explicit inputs and success criteria. A trial is one stochastic run of that task. A transcript (also called a trace or trajectory) is the complete record of the trial including all tool calls, reasoning steps, and intermediate results. The outcome is the final environmental state after the trial ends. A grader is the logic that scores whether the outcome meets the success criteria. A suite is the collection of tasks that constitutes your eval coverage. Most teams ship with none of these formalized. They have a demo that worked.

1

Grading the path, not the outcome

The most expensive mistake in agent eval design is checking whether the agent followed the expected tool-call sequence rather than whether it produced the correct result. In a demo, the agent follows the anticipated path and the team records that as success. In production, the agent finds a different valid path to the correct outcome. A path-checking grader flags this as failure. The team investigates the wrong thing for days.

Anthropic's guidance is unambiguous on this: grade what the agent produced, not the path it took to produce it. Agents in production find unanticipated valid approaches constantly. A grader that punishes them for it creates false failure signals that erode trust in the eval system itself.

2

Treating a single demo trial as evidence for a stochastic system

A single successful trial is not evidence of a production-ready agent. For user-facing tasks where consistency matters, the relevant metric is pass^k: the probability that all k trials succeed. An agent with a 70% per-trial success rate has a 34% probability of succeeding on 3 consecutive tasks. That is not a deployable system. Most teams do not have this number for their agents because most teams run exactly one trial per task in their evaluation process.

Contrast this with pass@k, which measures the probability that at least one of k trials succeeds. For use cases where any single success is acceptable, pass@k is the right signal. For user-facing flows where the agent needs to be reliably correct across multiple consecutive runs, pass^k is what tells you whether you are actually ready to ship.

3

Building graders after users report failures

On CORE-Bench, Anthropic's Opus 4.5 jumped from 42% to 95% success without any model change. The research team fixed grading bugs and tightened ambiguous task specifications. The model was right all along. The eval was wrong. Most teams building production agents today will never detect this class of error because they have no graders at all. If you have no graders, you cannot tell whether your agent is wrong or your eval is wrong. You are flying without instruments.

Anthropic recommends starting with 20 to 50 realistic tasks drawn from actual failures, not synthetic coverage. Write tasks where domain experts reach consistent verdicts. Include reference solutions that prove the task is solvable. That discipline is not extra work before launch. It is how you define what launch actually means.

The production reference. In building ObsrvAI, the first version of the anomaly-surfacing agent showed what felt like a strong demo pass rate on our internal test log streams. When we moved to production traffic with real alert volumes and tool-call chains longer than four steps, the pass^k metric on three consecutive trials dropped below 40%. We had no formal graders. We had no transcript collection. The first signal of failure was a user report. Rebuilding the eval harness after launch cost three weeks of engineering time that would have cost three days if we had started with the eval harness before shipping.

The PM's take. Your eval harness is your production acceptance criteria. If you cannot articulate what a passing outcome looks like for your three highest-stakes agent tasks, you are not ready to ship. The question is not "did the demo work?" The question is: what is the pass^k at k=5 for your highest-stakes user-facing flow?

Vinit's Take

The Discipline Gap Is the Production Gap

Editor's perspective

The MCP July 28 spec and Claude Opus 5 together mark the end of "infrastructure as excuse." The connectivity problem and the model-cost problem are solved for most teams building in 2026. By 2027, the teams that invested in eval infrastructure this year will have a compounding advantage: not because evals are magic, but because building an eval harness before launch forces you to operationalize what "working" means before users tell you what "broken" looks like. That discipline compounds into faster iteration, lower rework cost, and an eval suite that becomes a competitive asset. If your team is still treating evals as a post-launch instrumentation task, you are running the same roadmap that puts you in Gartner's 40%. The production bottleneck in 2026 is not the model. It is the measurement.
